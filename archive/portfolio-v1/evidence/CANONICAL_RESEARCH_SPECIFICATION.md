## 2. Research Context

### Setting
One bilingual primary school in China (English–Chinese medium of instruction). Grade 1–6 range across teacher participants.

### Problem Identified
Mo observed two patterns in his own classroom:
1. Same small group of English-confident pupils answering open-ended questions consistently while others stayed silent — including pupils who demonstrated understanding in Chinese-medium peer work.
2. Colleagues across the school spoke confidently about valuing open-ended questioning while lessons consistently reverted to closed, pace-driven exchanges.

### Why It Matters
The problem sits at the intersection of instructional quality (questioning as high-impact strategy) and equity (open-ended questioning as participation filter in linguistically uneven classrooms). The technique marketed as "inclusive" may do the opposite without adaptation.

### Justification
Personal classroom observation (repeated pattern), corroborated by awareness that this was school-wide, not one teacher's habit.

## 3. Methodology

### Design
Cross-sectional mixed-methods with triangulation across three data sources.

### Paradigm
Interpretivist (Mertens, 2019; Cohen et al., 2018); constructivist orientation to data collection/analysis (Opie & Brown, 2019).

### Participants
7 teachers completed questionnaire (mixed Likert + open-ended). 2 of 7 (Teacher A, Teacher B) also completed semi-structured interviews (~20 min each). All taught across both English-medium and Chinese-medium lessons for ≥1 full academic year.

### Data Collection Timeline
- **Week 1**: Questionnaire distribution and return (sealed envelopes)
- **Weeks 2–3**: Semi-structured interviews (Teacher A, Teacher B), written up within 1 hour
- **Weeks 4–5**: Two-pass thematic coding

### Data Sources
1. Questionnaire (n=7), Likert-scale items (Q4, Q7, Q9, Q12) + open-ended items
2. Semi-structured interviews (n=2)
3. Researcher reflexive memos

### Analysis Method
**Two-pass thematic coding** (Braun & Clarke, 2006):
- Pass 1: Open coding for surface patterns
- Pass 2: Interpretive pass for higher-order categories
- **Reliability rule**: Theme retained only if evidence in ≥2 independent sources
- **Credibility**: Triangulation across three data types + reflexive memoing for insider-researcher bias management

### Ethics
BERA (2018) guidelines; institutional approval before participant contact; written consent; participation information sheet; sealed-envelope return; no audio recording; password-protected storage; teacher codes A–G; raw data destroyed 6 months post-publication.

### Limitations
Self-report only; no classroom observation (not approved); only 2/7 interviewed; single school; n=7 (not statistically generalisable); insider-researcher position.

### Statistical Approach
Descriptive only. Explicit disclaimer: "Seven participants are too small a sample for inferential statistics; the numbers provide context for the qualitative data; they do not replace it."

## 4. Key Findings

### Finding 1: Structural Belief–Practice Gap
5 of 7 teachers showed mismatch between espoused belief (Q4A) and reported practice (Q4B). Gap attributed to institutional conditions (class size, pacing, absent policy), not knowledge failure.

### Finding 2: Open-Ended Questioning as Participation Filter
In bilingual classroom, open-ended questions consistently answered by same English-confident pupils; others stayed quiet, including those demonstrating understanding in Chinese. Alexander's (2020) dialogic framework under-accounted for this.

### Finding 3: English-Medium Questioning Conflates Production with Understanding
3 of 7 teachers independently described pupils who understood in Chinese but couldn't produce answers in English. BICS–CALP distinction shows risk of mis-assessing non-answers as non-understanding.

### Finding 4: Absence of Shared Institutional Framework
0 of 7 teachers referenced any questioning framework or bilingual questioning policy. Everyone solving same problem independently. This is a structural diagnosis.

### Finding 5: Personal Practice Change
Research changed Mo's response to silence — pausing to consider Chinese-language thinking before assuming non-understanding.

### Finding 6: Mixed Wait-Time Effects
Teacher B: extended pauses → more hands (consistent with Rowe, 1974). Teacher F: same stillness read as "something wrong" not productive thinking. Context-dependent.

### Finding 7: Methodological Rigour
Triangulation + documented reliability threshold + reflexive bias management demonstrated through practice.

## 8. Approved vs. Restricted Claims

### ✅ Approved Claims (Evidence Supports)
- Independent identification of classroom equity problem through systematic inquiry
- Critically evaluated established pedagogical framework (Alexander, 2020) for bilingual context
- Applied BICS–CALP theory to reframe assessment risk for bilingual learners
- Triangulated mixed-methods study with documented reliability threshold
- Reflexive management of insider-researcher bias
- Research changed personal classroom practice (pausing before assuming non-understanding)
- 5 of 7 teachers showed belief–practice gap
- 3 of 7 teachers described BICS–CALP conflation risk
- 0 of 7 teachers referenced shared questioning framework

### ⚠️ Restricted Claims (Use with Precise Framing)
- "Improved" or "enhanced" questioning (study identified gap, didn't fix it)
- "Differentiation awareness" (critiqued others' practice, not own technique)
- "Leadership potential" (diagnostic work, not led change)
- "Student impact" (no pupil data; teacher perception only)
- "Scalable findings" (n=7, single school, explicitly disclaimed generalisation)

### ❌ Restricted Claims (Do NOT Use)
- "Action research" — study is descriptive/interpretive, not intervention
- "Proved" or "demonstrated causal effect" — interpretivist design cannot prove
- "Statistically significant" — n=7 precludes inferential statistics
- "Pupil attainment improved" — no pupil data collected
- "School policy changed" — no evidence of institutional adoption
- "Achieved a distinction" — 75/100 not confirmed as distinction
- "Measurable impact on student outcomes" — explicitly no pupil data

## 9. Evidence Hierarchy

### Level 1 — Verified Facts
Direct quotes from source documents, recalculated statistics, explicit statements from essay and dataset. Cross-confirmed in ≥2 independent sources.

### Level 2 — Reasonable Inferences
Logical conclusions drawn from verified evidence (e.g., Country=China from "Chinese-medium context"). Tagged as [REASONABLE INFERENCE].

### Level 3 — Professional Interpretations
Analyst commentary and framing (e.g., "gap is structural, not knowledge failure"). Tagged as [PROFESSIONAL INTERPRETATION].

### Level 4 — Evidence Insufficient
Claims acknowledged as unsupported by available evidence (e.g., pupil outcomes, curriculum design). Tagged as "Evidence insufficient."
