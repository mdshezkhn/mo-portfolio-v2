## Finding 1: Structural Belief–Practice Gap

### Summary
Five of seven teachers demonstrated a mismatch between espoused belief (Q4A) and reported practice (Q4B) regarding questioning strategy. The gap is structural — stemming from pacing, class size, curriculum pressure, and absent institutional guidance — not a knowledge failure.

### Evidence
- Phase 1, Table 2: 5 of 7 teachers show Partial/Yes gap
- Phase 1, Table 1: Whole-class questioning (Q4f) highest mean at 4.7; open-ended (Q4b) at 3.9
- Phase 1, Section G: Essay conclusion links gap to institutional conditions

- [recruiter_translation.md](recruiter_translation.md) Finding 1
- [cv_assets.md](cv_assets.md) ATS Bullet 1, Leadership Bullet 1
- [overclaim_register.md](overclaim_register.md) Item 1 (4.9 error correction)

### Verification
**Confidence**: High
**Verified**: Recalculated independently from raw dataset, matches essay's Figure 1
**Source**: Phase 1 Quantitative Findings, Table 1 + Table 2

### Key Quotes

**Teacher A (Paraphrase)**: Resorting to closed questions due to pacing and class size (30 pupils) pressure to keep the lesson moving.

**Teacher B (Paraphrase)**: Open-ended questions in English consistently draw responses only from a small group of English-confident pupils.

**Analytical Conclusion**: The belief–practice gap is structural, not a knowledge failure — it stems from pacing, curriculum pressure, class size, and the complete absence of institutional guidance, not from teachers not knowing better (Argyris & Schön, 1974; Borg, 2019).

## Finding 2: Open-Ended Questioning as Participation Filter

### Summary
Open-ended questioning, in a bilingual context with uneven English proficiency, functioned in teachers' own accounts as a participation filter rather than an inclusion strategy. The same English-confident pupils tended to answer while others stayed quiet.

### Evidence
- Phase 1, Qualitative Findings: Teacher quotes from A and B
- Phase 1, Section G: Essay critique of Alexander (2020)
- Phase 3, Impact Register Row 2
- Phase 4, Recruiter Translation Finding 2

### Cross-References
- [findings.md](findings.md) Finding 2
- [impact_register.md](impact_register.md) Row 2
- [recruiter_translation.md](recruiter_translation.md) Finding 2
- [cv_assets.md](cv_assets.md) ATS Bullet 1
- [overclaim_register.md](overclaim_register.md) Item 8 (not "proven" exclusionary)

### Verification
**Confidence**: High
**Verified**: Consistent across 5 of 7 teachers + both interviewees
**Source**: Phase 1 Qualitative Findings; essay §4.2; dataset Thematic Coding T6

### Key Quotes

**Teacher B (Paraphrase)**: Open-ended questions in English consistently draw responses only from a small group of English-confident pupils.

**Teacher A (Paraphrase)**: Noted that open-ended questions can lead to less participation from quieter students.

**Analytical Conclusion**: Alexander's (2020) dialogic teaching framework was not built for classrooms where linguistic confidence is unevenly distributed. Applied without adaptation, it may widen participation gaps rather than narrow them.

## Finding 3: English-Medium Questioning and Conceptual Understanding (BICS–CALP)

### Summary
Multiple teachers described students who demonstrated conceptual understanding in Chinese but could not produce corresponding answers in English. This suggests English-only questioning risks conflating language production with conceptual understanding (Cummins's 1984 BICS–CALP distinction).

### Evidence
- Phase 1, Section F: Qualitative findings with teacher quotes
- Phase 3, Impact Register Row 3 (highest recruiter value)
- Phase 4, Recruiter Translation Finding 3
- Cross-referenced by 3 of 7 teachers (A, B, E) independently

### Verification
**Confidence**: High (strongest and most consequential finding)
**Verified**: Named explicitly by 3 of 7 teachers (A, B, E) independently

### Key Quotes

**Teacher B (Paraphrase)**: Acknowledged the risk of misclassifying language production limitations as conceptual deficits.

## Finding 4: Absence of Shared Institutional Framework

### Summary
Not one of the 7 teachers referenced a formal questioning framework, school-level policy on questioning in bilingual lessons, or any shared institutional guidance on managing language diversity in classroom interaction. This absence held consistently across both questionnaire and interview data.

### Evidence
- Phase 1, Section F: Qualitative Negative Finding
- Phase 3, Impact Register Row 4 (high leadership value)
- Phase 4, Recruiter Translation — Absence Finding

### Cross-References
- [findings.md](findings.md) Finding 4
- [impact_register.md](impact_register.md) Row 4
- [recruiter_translation.md](recruiter_translation.md) Absence Finding
- [cv_assets.md](cv_assets.md) Leadership Bullet 3

### Verification
**Confidence**: High
**Verified**: Universal absence across all 7 questionnaires + both interviews
**Source**: Phase 1 Qualitative Findings; Thematic Coding T11; Interpretive Coding item 21

### Implications
This is a structural finding: the belief–practice gap persists because no shared framework exists. Every teacher was independently solving the same problem — individual trial-and-error rather than collective practice.

## Finding 5: Personal Practice Change

### Summary
Mo changed his own classroom response to student silence directly as a result of the research. When a pupil doesn't answer, he now pauses and considers whether they might be thinking in Chinese before assuming they don't understand.

### Evidence
- Phase 2: Reflective practice entry (High confidence)
- Phase 3, Impact Register Row 5
- Phase 4, Recruiter Translation (STAR Story 3)
- Phase 7, Portfolio Case Study Conclusion
- Essay Conclusion (direct quote)

### Key Quote
*"This study shifted how I respond to silence in my own classroom. When a student does not answer, I now pause and ask myself whether they might be thinking in Chinese. Often something comes shortly after, in the language they know best."*

### Verification
**Confidence**: Moderate (self-reported, single instance, not independently observed)
**Source**: Essay Conclusion; Phase 2 Reflective Practice entry

## Finding 6: Mixed Wait-Time Effects

### Summary
Wait-time effects were not consistent across teachers. Teacher B found extended pauses produced more hands and better answers (consistent with Rowe, 1974); Teacher F found the same stillness was read by pupils as "something has gone wrong," not as productive thinking time.

### Evidence
- Phase 1, Section F: Unexpected/contradictory findings
- Phase 3, Impact Register Row 7

### Verification
**Confidence**: Moderate (context-bound, n=7)
**Source**: Phase 1 Qualitative Findings; interview transcripts

## Notable: Q9 Scale Compression (NOT a Finding)

All 7 teachers rated student confidence at exactly 3/5. The essay itself flags this as almost certainly scale compression rather than genuine consensus — NOT a positive result. This should never be cited as a finding.

### Verification
**Confidence**: High (as a cautionary note; LOW as cited evidence of students)
**Source**: Phase 1, Table 1; Phase 10, Evidence Register Row 10

## Notable: 4.9 vs 4.7 Correction

**⚠ Correction**: The essay's discussion prose states the whole-class questioning mean (Q4f) was **4.9**. Recalculating directly from the raw Likert scores in the dataset (5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 3) gives a mean of **4.7** — which matches the essay's own Figure 1 bar chart exactly. **The verified, defensible number is 4.7.**
