## Risk Level Definitions

### High Risk
- Directly falsifiable if probed (e.g., "how did you measure pupil impact?")
- Contradicts explicit disclaimers in source material
- Undermines credibility if questioned

### Moderate-High Risk
- Often probed directly in interviews (e.g., leadership claims)
- Requires specific evidence that's absent
- Creates credibility gaps when challenged

### Moderate Risk
- Misrepresents methodology or interpretation
- Overstates what study design supports
- Can be corrected with precise reframing

### Low-Moderate Risk
- Minor factual errors (e.g., wrong number)
- May be caught by detail-oriented reviewers
- Typically doesn't destroy credibility but weakens accuracy

## Key Risk Areas by Phase

### Phase 1-2: Quantitative Misinterpretation
- Risk: Using 4.9 instead of 4.7 for Q4f mean
- Risk: Claiming statistical significance or effect sizes
- Risk: Misinterpreting uniform Q9=3/5 as positive finding

### Phase 3: Impact Overclaim
- Risk: Stating pupil impact or school implementation
- Risk: Confusing diagnostic findings with implemented solutions
- Risk: Overstating transferability beyond context

### Phase 4-5: Evidence Misrepresentation
- Risk: Overstating leadership or curriculum evidence
- Risk: Blurring research-instrument assessment with pupil assessment
- Risk: Inflating continuous improvement evidence

### Phase 6-7: Narrative Embellishment
- Risk: Overstating personal practice change as systemic impact
- Risk: Implying STAR stories represent measured outcomes
- Risk: Suggesting reflection alone caused change without evidence

### Phase 8-9: Marketing Language
- Risk: Inventing percentages where only raw counts exist
- Risk: Adding outcomes where only processes documented
- Risk: Suggesting broader adoption where none evidenced

### Phase 10-12: Synthesis Errors
- Risk: Citing unverified claims from earlier phases
- Risk: Creating new implications not in source material
- Risk: Overgeneralizing beyond single-school context

## Overall Audit Conclusion

The genuine risk is **register drift**: letting the careful, hedged language of an interpretivist study slide into confident, outcome-oriented language recruiters expect. Safest approach: lead with real strength (rigorous, honest, small-scale practitioner inquiry producing useful, non-obvious findings) rather than reaching for unsupported bigger claims.

**Safe Pitch**: "I noticed a real equity problem in my own bilingual classroom, investigated it rigorously and honestly across seven colleagues, found a technique assumed to be inclusive wasn't functioning that way — and it changed how I teach."

Anything more ambitious (measured pupil impact, led school change, action research, statistical significance) requires sourcing from elsewhere in evidence base.
