## 300-Word Article

**When "Inclusive" Questioning Isn't: What I Found Researching My Own Bilingual Classroom**

For years I assumed, like most teachers trained in dialogic teaching, that open-ended questions were the more inclusive choice. They invite multiple answers. They make room for deeper thinking. Then I started noticing something in my own bilingual school: the same small group of English-confident pupils answered every open-ended question, while others — some of whom clearly understood the material when I watched them work with a Chinese-speaking peer — stayed quiet.

I decided to investigate properly rather than trust my own impression. Seven colleagues, all experienced across both English-medium and Chinese-medium lessons, completed a questionnaire; two took part in semi-structured interviews. What came back confirmed the pattern was real and shared, not just something I'd noticed. Five of seven teachers described open-ended questions being answered by the same few pupils every time. One put it plainly: "It is always the same three or four kids — the ones who are comfortable in English. The rest just wait."

The deeper finding was about assessment, not technique. Four of seven teachers independently described pupils who understood a concept in Chinese but couldn't yet say it in English — and one admitted she'd sometimes read that silence as the pupil not understanding. That's a real risk: mistaking a language-production gap for a knowledge gap.

Just as telling was what nobody mentioned. Not one of the seven teachers could point to a shared school framework for questioning in bilingual lessons. Everyone was solving the same problem alone.

This research has genuinely changed how I teach. When a pupil goes quiet now, I pause and ask myself whether they might be thinking in Chinese before I assume they don't know the answer. Small change. Real difference.

*(Master's-level practitioner research, University of Cumbria, 75/100)*

## 120-Word Project Summary

Small-scale practitioner research at my own bilingual primary school, examining how questioning strategies function when English proficiency is unevenly distributed across a class. Seven teachers completed a mixed questionnaire; two took part in semi-structured interviews. Key finding: open-ended questioning, usually assumed to be more inclusive, tended to filter participation toward the most English-confident pupils, while others — some of whom understood the material in Chinese — went unheard. A second finding, drawing on Cummins's BICS–CALP framework: English-only questioning risks mistaking a language-production gap for a lack of understanding. No teacher referenced any shared school framework for bilingual questioning practice. Master's-level, University of Cumbria (75/100).
