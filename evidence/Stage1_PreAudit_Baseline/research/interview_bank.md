## Story 1: Spotting and Investigating a Real Equity Problem

### Situation
In my own bilingual primary school, I kept noticing the same small group of English-confident pupils answering every open-ended question, while others — including pupils who clearly understood the material when working with a Chinese-speaking peer — stayed silent. Colleagues across the school also talked confidently about valuing open-ended, dialogic questioning, yet lessons consistently reverted to closed, pace-driven exchanges.

### Task
As part of my PGCE practitioner research module, I needed to investigate whether this was a real, shared pattern or just my own perception, and understand why it was happening.

### Actions
I designed a mixed-methods study: a Likert-and-open-ended questionnaire for seven colleagues who had all taught across both English-medium and Chinese-medium lessons, followed by semi-structured interviews with two of them. I secured institutional ethical approval, used sealed-envelope returns to reduce social-desirability bias, and applied a two-pass thematic coding process with a documented reliability rule (a theme only counted if it appeared in at least two independent sources).

### Results
The research confirmed the pattern was real and shared, not just my own perception: five of seven teachers showed a gap between what they said they valued and what they reported actually doing in lessons. Just as importantly, not one of the seven referenced any shared school framework or guidance for questioning in bilingual lessons — suggesting the gap was structural, not a knowledge failure.

### Reflection
The most useful thing I learned wasn't the gap itself — it was that the gap persisted *even among teachers who were already aware of it*. That told me the fix isn't more individual training; it's shared institutional guidance that makes the harder practice feel manageable under real classroom pressure.

**Evidence used**: Essay §3.2-4.1; Phase 1 Table 2; dataset raw Likert scores.

### Audience Versions

**Leadership**
I use this as a case study for why CPD focused purely on technique often fails — the absence of shared institutional guidance is, in my experience, a bigger lever than individual teacher training. If I were leading CPD or curriculum decisions, this is the kind of diagnostic work I'd want done before designing an intervention, not after.

**Teaching**
[As written above — this is the natural register for a classroom-teacher interview.]

**Middle-Leadership**
This research gave me a concrete example of diagnosing a whole-team issue systematically rather than assuming I already knew the cause. If I were coordinating a phase or subject team, I'd want to replicate this kind of quick, low-stakes survey-plus-conversation approach before proposing any policy change — it surfaces whether a problem is individual or structural, which changes what the right response is.

### Likely Interview Questions
- Tell me about a time you identified a problem in your own practice and investigated it.
- Describe a piece of research or reflective practice that changed how you think about teaching.
- How do you approach equity issues in a multilingual classroom?

## Story 2: Challenging My Own Assumption About "Inclusive" Teaching

### Situation
Like most teachers who've done CPD on dialogic teaching, I assumed open-ended questioning was the more inclusive choice — they invite multiple answers and deeper thinking. My research findings challenged that assumption directly.

### Task
I needed to establish, honestly, whether open-ended questioning was actually working equitably in a classroom with uneven English proficiency, rather than just assuming the literature's claims applied unchanged to a bilingual setting.

### Actions
I asked colleagues directly, through both the questionnaire and interviews, what actually happened when they used open-ended questions in mixed-proficiency classes. I didn't lead them toward a particular answer — the questionnaire simply asked which strategies they believed were most effective and which they reported using most, and let the gap emerge from the data itself.

### Results
Five of seven teachers described the same pattern: open-ended questions were consistently answered by the same three or four English-confident pupils, while others — including pupils who understood the material — stayed quiet. One colleague put it directly in interview: "The rest just wait, or look at each other." This directly contradicted the assumption that open-ended questioning is automatically more inclusive.

### Reflection
This was the finding that most changed my own thinking. I now treat "open-ended = more inclusive" as a claim to test in a specific context, not an assumption to import unchanged — especially in a linguistically mixed classroom, where the format of a question can determine who gets heard as much as the quality of their thinking does.

**Evidence used**: Essay §4.2; Finding 2, Phases 1/3/4.

### Likely Interview Questions
- Tell me about a time your research or experience challenged something you believed.
- How do you make questioning genuinely inclusive in a mixed-proficiency classroom?
- Describe a time you had to update your own practice based on evidence.

### Audience Versions

**Leadership**
If a school is investing in dialogic-teaching CPD, this finding is exactly the kind of thing I'd want surfaced before rollout — a framework that works well in a monolingual context can have unintended equity effects in a bilingual one unless it's deliberately adapted.

**Middle-Leadership**
This gives me a concrete, evidence-based reason to push back constructively on any CPD initiative that assumes a technique will transfer unchanged into a bilingual or EAL-heavy context — and a way to frame that pushback with data rather than just opinion.

## Story 3: Rethinking How I Assess Understanding in EAL Learners

### Situation
Several colleagues I interviewed described a specific, uncomfortable pattern: pupils who clearly understood a concept in Chinese but went blank when asked to answer in English — and one colleague admitted she had, at times, read that silence as the pupil "not getting it."

### Task
I needed to understand whether this was a genuine, shared risk in how we assess bilingual learners, or an isolated perception.

### Actions
I cross-referenced this pattern across the questionnaire's open-ended responses and both interviews, applying Cummins's (1984) BICS–CALP distinction — the idea that conversational fluency develops faster than the academic language needed to demonstrate understanding aloud — to interpret what I was seeing.

### Results
Four of the seven teachers described the same underlying issue in their own words, independently. This became, in my own assessment, the most consequential finding of the whole study: English-only questioning risks conflating language production with conceptual understanding.

### Reflection
This finding directly changed my own classroom practice. When a pupil doesn't answer now, I pause and consider whether they might be thinking in Chinese before assuming they don't understand — a small change, but one grounded directly in what this research showed me.

**Evidence used**: Essay §4.3; Finding 3, Phases 1/3/4; essay conclusion (personal practice change).

### Likely Interview Questions
- How do you check for understanding with EAL or bilingual learners?
- Tell me about a time research changed a specific thing you do in the classroom.
- What's a mistake or blind spot you've corrected in your own teaching?

### Audience Versions

**Leadership**
This finding is directly usable as an input to a school's formative assessment policy for bilingual learners — specifically, building in a simple practice of distinguishing "understands but can't yet say it in English" from genuine non-understanding.

**Middle-Leadership**
If I were coordinating EAL provision or moderation practice, this is the kind of finding I'd want built into shared assessment guidance — not as a one-off insight, but as a standing check built into how staff read pupil silence.

## Story 4: Managing My Own Bias as an Insider Researcher

### Situation
Because I was researching my own school and colleagues, there was a real risk that my existing views would shape what I saw and reported.

### Task
I needed a way to manage that risk honestly, rather than simply asserting objectivity I couldn't fully claim.

### Actions
I kept reflexive memos after each interview specifically to flag moments where my own perspective might be steering the data. One documented example: after probing a colleague about wait-time, I noted that I might have been "pulling the question toward time-management rather than language equity," and went back to the raw transcript rather than trusting my in-the-moment interpretation.

### Results
This process led me to revisit and correct at least one piece of my own analysis rather than let an unexamined assumption stand. It also meant that when questionnaire and interview data diverged, I had a documented practice of returning to the raw data rather than defaulting to whichever account confirmed what I already believed.

### Reflection
Research on your own workplace is only as credible as your honesty about your own blind spots. I'd rather document a moment of possible bias and correct for it than produce findings that look clean but aren't trustworthy.

**Evidence used**: Essay §3.1, dated memo example (28 Feb 2025).

### Likely Interview Questions
- How do you make sure your own opinions don't distort your judgement about a colleague or a situation?
- Tell me about a time you had to check your own bias.
- Describe your approach to self-evaluation or reflective practice.

### Audience Versions

**Leadership**
This is directly relevant to leading any self-evaluation process — a school's own staff investigating their own practice always carries this same risk, and I have a concrete, practised method for managing it rather than just asserting good intentions.

**Middle-Leadership**
If I were leading a team through a self-evaluation cycle, I'd build in the same discipline — documented reflexive checks and a rule to return to raw evidence when interpretations diverge — rather than relying on individual goodwill to keep the process honest.
