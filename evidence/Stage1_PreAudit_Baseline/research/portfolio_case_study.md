# Portfolio Case Study - PGCE7002 Practitioner Research

## A Critical Reflection on Questioning Strategies in a Bilingual Primary School

### The Challenge
In my own Grade 1–6 bilingual primary school, where lessons move between English and Chinese, I kept noticing the same pattern: when I asked an open-ended question, the same small group of English-confident pupils answered, while others — some of whom I knew understood the material perfectly well from watching them work with a Chinese-speaking peer — stayed silent. There was a second pattern, harder to name at first. Colleagues across the school spoke confidently about the value of open-ended, dialogic questioning. Lessons still consistently defaulted to closed, pace-driven exchanges. It wasn't one teacher's habit. It happened everywhere, and it was worth understanding properly.

### Context
This was practitioner research conducted as a full-time teacher at the school being studied — a position that gave me genuine familiarity with the setting, but also required real care around my own interpretive bias. All seven teachers I eventually surveyed had taught across both English-medium and Chinese-medium lessons for at least a full academic year, giving the group a shared, relevant frame of reference for the questions I wanted to ask.

### Research Questions
Three questions shaped the inquiry: (1) What gap, if any, exists between teachers' stated beliefs about questioning and their reported classroom practice? (2) Does open-ended questioning work equitably in a bilingual primary classroom? (3) How does English-medium questioning shape which learners appear to understand and which do not?

### Approach
I adopted an interpretivist paradigm, because I wanted to understand the *meanings* teachers attached to their questioning choices, not simply count how often particular strategies occurred. Seven colleagues completed a mixed questionnaire combining Likert-scale items with open-ended questions, returned in sealed envelopes to reduce social-desirability pressure. Two of those seven took part in semi-structured interviews of around twenty minutes each, written up in full within an hour of each session — no audio recording, in line with the ethical agreement I'd secured. The whole process was conducted in-house Institute for Education guidelines.

### Implementation
Questionnaires went out and came back in Week 1. Interviews with Teacher A and Teacher B ran across Weeks 2 and 3, written up immediately afterwards. Thematic analysis followed in Weeks 4 and 5, using a two-pass process: an initial open-coding pass for surface patterns, then an interpretive pass to develop higher-order analytical categories. I treated a theme as analytically sound only when it appeared in at least two independent sources — questionnaire and interview data cross-checked against each other rather than taken at face value. Throughout, I kept reflexive memos specifically to catch moments where my own perspective might be shaping the analysis. One entry, from 28 February 2025, notes that I may have been "pulling the question toward time-management rather than language equity" while probing Teacher A about wait-time — a flag that sent me back to the raw transcript rather than my in-the-moment read of it.

### Evidence

Three findings anchored the study, each triangulated across at least two independent data sources:

1. **A structural belief–practice gap.** Five of seven teachers showed a mismatch between what they said they valued in questioning (mostly open-ended and higher-order questions) and what they reported actually doing in lessons (mostly closed, whole-class exchanges). Recalculating the raw Likert data confirmed this: whole-class questioning had the highest mean score of any strategy in the dataset (4.7), while open-ended questioning scored notably lower (3.9) despite being the strategy most teachers said they valued most.

2. **Open-ended questioning as an unintended participation filter.** Five of seven teachers described the same underlying pattern — that open-ended questions in English tended to be answered by the same few English-confident pupils, while others, including pupils who understood the material, stayed quiet. One colleague put it directly: "When I ask an open question in English, I know who is going to answer. It is always the same three or four kids." This runs against the common assumption, embedded in influential frameworks like Alexander's (2020) dialogic teaching model, that open-ended questioning is inherently more inclusive.

3. **English-medium questioning obscuring genuine understanding.** Four of seven teachers independently described pupils who clearly understood a concept in Chinese but could not yet produce the answer in English — and one admitted candidly that she had, at times, read that silence as the pupil simply not understanding. Read through Cummins's (1984) BICS–CALP distinction, this suggested a real risk: that English-only questioning can quietly conflate language production with conceptual understanding.

A fourth, structural finding ran underneath all three: not one of the seven teachers, across either data source, referenced any shared school framework or policy for questioning in bilingual lessons. Everyone was solving the same problem independently.

### Results
The research confirmed that what I'd been noticing wasn't just my own perception — it was a real, shared pattern across the staff group, and one with a clear underlying cause: the complete absence of any shared institutional guidance on bilingual questioning practice. It also produced one concrete change in my own classroom. I now pause, when a pupil doesn't answer, and consider whether they might be thinking in Chinese before assuming they don't understand — a small shift, but one directly traceable to what this research showed me.

I want to be precise about what this study does *not* claim. It did not measure pupil outcomes — classroom observation wasn't approved, and no pupil assessment data was collected. With seven participants, it explicitly does not claim statistical significance; the Likert data is used descriptively, to identify convergence and contradiction, not to prove anything numerically. This was, and is presented as, a small-scale, context-specific piece of practitioner inquiry — not a generalisable outcome study.

### Reflection
The most important thing I learned wasn't any single finding — it was that the belief–practice gap persisted *even among teachers who were already aware of it*. That told me the usual response to this kind of gap — more individual CPD on questioning technique — is aimed at the wrong target. If every teacher is solving the same problem alone, with no shared framework to draw on, the gap is a systems problem, not a knowledge problem.

I also had to be honest with myself about the limits of researching my own workplace. Keeping reflexive memos wasn't a formality — it genuinely changed how I read at least one piece of data, and it's a discipline I'd want to carry into any future inquiry I lead.

### Lessons Learned
- A widely-cited pedagogical framework (Alexander's dialogic teaching model) can have real, unintended equity effects when applied without adaptation to a linguistically uneven classroom.
- Silence in a bilingual classroom needs to be read carefully — it can mean genuine non-understanding, or it can mean a pupil is translating in their head and needs more time or a different route to show what they know.
- A belief–practice gap that persists despite teacher awareness is a signal to look at conditions and institutional support, not at individual teacher competence.

### Professional Growth
This research sharpened two things in my own practice directly: how I read silence after a question, and how sceptical I am now of assuming a technique marketed as "inclusive" actually functions that way for every learner in a specific context. It also gave me direct, practised experience in triangulating data honestly and managing my own bias as an insider researcher — skills I'd bring to any future school self-evaluation or inquiry-based improvement work.

### Future Application
The essay itself proposes video-recorded lessons as the next logical step — testing whether translanguaging-informed adjustments (posing a question in Chinese first, allowing brief peer discussion, then asking for an English synthesis) actually change who participates and how. I see this as a natural next phase of my own professional inquiry, and a concrete, evidence-based starting point for any school-level conversation about bilingual questioning practice.
